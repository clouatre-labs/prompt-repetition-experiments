\begin{abstract}
\vspace{3mm}
\textbf{Context:} Prompt repetition, the verbatim duplication of an input transforming
\texttt{<QUERY>} into \texttt{<QUERY><QUERY>}, has been shown to improve accuracy for
non-reasoning large language models (LLMs) on retrieval and multiple-choice benchmarks.
\textbf{Objective:} We ask at the class level whether non-reasoning LLM agents in multi-step
pipelines gain task success from a verbatim second copy of their instructions.
\textbf{Method:} A pilot series of four exploratory experiments (Part I; Claude Haiku 4.5 and
Mercury 2.5 delegates, $n{=}5$ per condition, binary rubric scoring by a single LLM judge that was
not independently validated) motivated a pre-registered confirmatory experiment with model as a
factor (Part II). Experiment~5 ran two non-reasoning models, \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} and
\texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}, with $n{=}20$ per arm and model (80 runs), executable fractional
scoring on the primary outcome \texttt{primary\_total = C3 + C4 + C5} (0 to 3), and a
pre-registration deposited before the first run (\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24611615}{DOI:10.5281/zenodo.24611615}).
\textbf{Results:} After 10 pre-registered contamination exclusions, 36 control and 34 treatment
runs remain. The rank-biserial effect of repetition is $r{=}0.1667$ (bootstrap 95\% CI
$[-0.2254, 0.5494]$, $p{=}0.4002$) for \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} and $r{=}-0.0069$
($[-0.3681, 0.3958]$, $p{=}0.9862$) for \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}; both intervals include zero.
\textbf{Conclusions:} We detect no effect of prompt repetition on executable task success for
either model. The pilot series adds a documented rubric-runner co-design failure and the
containment requirements that made the confirmatory run possible; all data are open.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{4mm}

\section{Introduction}

\citet{leviathan2025} demonstrated that verbatim repetition of the input, transforming
\texttt{<QUERY>} into \texttt{<QUERY>\allowbreak<QUERY>}, improves accuracy for
non-reasoning large language models (LLMs) across a wide range of benchmarks. The intervention wins 47 of 70
benchmark-model combinations with zero losses, gaining up to 76 percentage points (pp) on positional
retrieval tasks such as NameIndex.
The mechanism is efficient: repeated tokens produce no additional output and impose
no mandatory latency penalty on most providers.

Multi-step LLM-agent pipelines constitute a structurally different evaluation setting.
Agents execute multi-step tool-call loops, accumulate context across hundreds of messages,
and produce structured artifacts graded against rubrics.
Whether applying the analogous repetition to fixed delegate instructions produces similar gains
in multi-step agentic pipelines remains untested.

The publicly available AIDev dataset \citep{li2026aidev} captures approximately one million
agent-authored pull requests from real-world GitHub repositories; our study complements this
observational corpus with a controlled laboratory design that enables causal inference over a
single variable (instruction repetition) while holding task, model, and temperature constant.

\textbf{RQ:} Does prompt repetition (the duplicate-query pattern applied to delegate instructions) improve task-success rates for non-reasoning LLM agents in multi-step pipelines, and does the answer depend on the model?

The paper has two parts. Part~I is a pilot series of four exploratory experiments (Exp1 to Exp4) with one model each and a single LLM judge; the research question was formulated and registered before any experiment was run \citep{kitchenham2002guidelines}, but the pilots were underpowered and their judge was not independently validated, so they are used only to motivate the design of Part~II. Part~II is the pre-registered, powered confirmatory experiment (Exp5) with model as a factor, executable scoring and a deposited protocol \citep{clouatre2026prereg}.

This paper contributes: (1) the Exp5 result, a null effect of prompt repetition on executable task success for two non-reasoning models at $n{=}20$ per arm; (2) a formal characterization of \emph{rubric-runner co-design failure} as a distinct evaluation failure mode, shown in Exp3 and addressed by the runner-prompt manipulation of Exp4; and (3) open datasets of session logs, scoring records, executable scorers and pre-registration materials archived at \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19696593}{DOI:10.5281/zenodo.19696593} \citep{clouatre2026dataset}, enabling replication and secondary analysis. Negative results remain rare in software engineering research despite explicit calls to value them \citep{paige2017negative}. Without published null results, a positive finding risks being generalized to untested settings \citep{wieringa2015generalizing}; we report this result to constrain that generalization.

This work addresses methodological challenges in the empirical evaluation of agentic software systems: failure patterns in agentic pipelines (rubric-runner co-design failure), and testing and evaluation of AI agents (pre-registered executable scoring pipeline).

\section{Related Work}


Independently, \citet{qian2026} show that benchmark saturation varies sharply by task: MATH-500
reaches a discriminability score of 0.16 while AIME 2024 reaches 0.74. Ceiling effects
suppress treatment signals regardless of intervention strength.
\citet{chen2026causal} demonstrate, via causal prompt optimization, that near-ceiling queries produce no detectable treatment signal regardless of prompt quality; all gains concentrate on hard queries. This mirrors Experiments~1 and~2: below the discriminability threshold, outcome variance is too low for any intervention to register.

\citet{zheng2023} establish LLM-as-judge as a practical but validity-limited approach, recommending
inter-rater reliability measures, and report over 80\% agreement between GPT-4
judgments and human preferences on MT-Bench, but note position bias. \citet{shen2023} find that single-judge alignment with human raters is candidate-, dimension-, and quality-dependent, with per-system correlation spread of up to 0.5; rubric-based scoring with judge calibration has been proposed to address this variability \citep{hashemi2024llmrubric}.
The MSR~2026 Mining Challenge applied AIDev to characterise agent failure taxonomies, task-stratified acceptance, and review effort \citep{failureMSR2026,comparingMSR2026,revieweffortMSR2026}; none of these observational studies isolates instruction repetition as a controlled pre-registered independent variable.
The pilot series (Part~I) uses a single Haiku~4.5 judge; a post hoc second-judge check is reported in Section~\ref{subsec:scoring-validation}, but it was not an independent re-scoring and leaves judge reliability untested. Part~II replaces the judge with an executable scorer.
\citet{adaptorch2026} report that static parallel agent topologies
achieve competitive results on SWE-bench Verified under greedy decoding. This supports the
delegate design used here. Substantive null results from sound designs are distinct from
methodological failures \citep{sculley2018}: a sound design delimits
intervention scope; a flawed design reveals evaluation infrastructure defects.

\section{Part I: Pilot Series (Experiments 1 to 4)}
\label{sec:partI}

Experiments~1 to~4 are exploratory. Each used one delegate model, $n{=}5$ per condition, and binary rubric scoring by a single LLM judge (Claude Haiku~4.5) that was not independently validated; the second-judge check of Section~\ref{subsec:scoring-validation} is a score-reconstruction exercise only. Part~I scores are therefore used only to motivate the design changes of Part~II and carry no confirmatory weight.

The treatment mirrors the \texttt{<QUERY><QUERY>} pattern of \citet{leviathan2025} applied to
the delegate system prompt rather than to a single-turn query (\autoref{fig:pipeline}). Goose versions 1.25.0 (Exp1,

\subsubsection{Pre-registration Protocol}
\label{sec:preregistration}

The evaluation rubric was locked before any delegate was spawned. Group assignments were sealed
in a \texttt{label-map.json} file before scoring began, with file creation timestamps serving
as the pre-registration record, archived in the supplementary dataset \citep{clouatre2026dataset}.

A separate Haiku~4.5 instance performed the scoring.
It received the delegate output JSON and the rubric criteria. In Experiments~2 and~3 the output
files were named neutrally (\texttt{scout-run-NN}), so no group labels, run metadata, or other
outputs were visible. In Experiment~1 the scoring prompt listed the files by condition
(\texttt{scout-control-N}, \texttt{scout-treatment-N}) and noted that one control run had failed,
so the scorer knew each run's condition.

\subsubsection{Scoring Validation}
\label{subsec:scoring-validation}

A post hoc second-judge check was run over a stratified sample of 5 sessions before journal submission. A second LLM judge (Claude Sonnet~4.6) was given the rubric criteria, the session's condition, and the primary judge's (Claude Haiku~4.5) total score, and was asked to reproduce the per-criterion scores. The sample included one control and one treatment session from Experiment~1 (FastMCP refactor), one from each condition in Experiment~2 (Tree-sitter abstract syntax tree (AST) scanner), and one treatment session from Experiment~3 (Kotlin grammar). Per-criterion agreement between the two judges was 100\% ($\kappa = 1.00$, 33 criteria across 5 sessions). Session-level pass/fail agreement was also 100\%, though Cohen's $\kappa$ was undefined because all sessions were rated as passing the 50\% threshold by both judges. The second judge did not see the delegate outputs. This check is therefore a score-reconstruction exercise, not an independent re-scoring: it shows only that the two judges' criterion-level scores were consistent given the totals, and it provides no evidence that the primary judge's scores are reproducible from the delegate outputs alone. Judge reliability is consequently untested (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

The inferential test (Mann-Whitney U, two-tailed, $\alpha{=}0.05$) and effect size metric
(rank-biserial $r$ via \citep{kerby2014}) were pre-specified from Exp2 onward. For Exp1, Fisher's exact test was used post-hoc when the valid-run count fell to $n{=}4$ control and $n{=}5$ treatment.



\paragraph{Statistical power.}
\label{par:power}
At $n{=}5$ per group, Mann-Whitney U achieves 80\% power only for near-complete separation
effects ($r \geq 0.80$, equivalent to one group dominating the other by 4:1 or more).
Detecting a medium effect ($r{=}0.50$) requires $n \approx 20$ per group \citep{faul2007}.
Infrastructure cost constrained the sample size to 5 runs per group; the experiments are therefore best
interpreted as pilot studies capable of detecting large effects or complete separations, not
medium effects.

\subsection{Experiment 4}
\label{subsec:exp4design}

Experiment~4 targeted issue \texttt{aptu-coder\#648},\footnote{\url{https://github.com/clouatre-labs/aptu-coder/issues/648}} Swift grammar support
via tree-sitter-swift. It changed three things relative to Experiments~1 to~3. The delegate was \texttt{inception/mercury-2.5-preview} via OpenRouter
(temperature 0.5, Goose 1.49.0). The treatment definition differed: the entire user \texttt{prompt} block of the recipe was duplicated verbatim and the
system \texttt{instructions} were unchanged, whereas Experiments~1 to~3 repeat the Scout instructions. And the runner prompt was manipulated directly:
the Scout instructions were rewritten (prompt v2, then v3) and the rubric revised (v2, then v3) between three $n{=}1{+}1$ pilot rounds that scored
0/7, 3/7 and 5/7. The runner-prompt manipulation is the first response to the rubric-runner co-design failure documented in Section~\ref{subsec:codesign};
because the repeated element, the model and the framework version all differ, Exp4 is a different manipulation and its results are not comparable to those of Exp1 to Exp3.
The rubric was authored over the revision rounds and was not inter-rater checked before the main run ($n{=}5{+}5$, runs 07 to 16; per-run table in Appendix~\ref{app:exp4}).

\subsection{Results of Experiments 1 to 3}
\label{sec:results}

\subsubsection{Pre-analysis Amendments}
\label{sec:amendments}

After scoring was complete, criteria C1, C5, and C6 each scored 0/1 across both groups in
Experiment~3. Inspection of the runner prompt (\texttt{runner-prompt.md}) confirmed that it
contained no instruction directing agents to investigate ABI compatibility between
\texttt{tree-sitter-kotlin} 0.3.8 and \texttt{tree-sitter} 0.26.6 (C1), to write test cases
exercising \texttt{.kts} file parsing (C5), or to inspect the \texttt{LanguageInfo} struct
fields or \texttt{aptu-coder}\#649 to determine whether a \texttt{DEFUSE\_QUERY} constant was required (C6).

Both groups scored identically (0/1) on C1, C5, and C6, so excluding them cannot bias the treatment comparison in either direction. The exclusion was determined after scoring but before any group labels were inspected, aligning with registered-report amendment practice \citep{ernst2023registered}. All three criteria are retained in the full rubric table (see Appendix~\ref{app:rubrics}); the primary comparison is restricted to C2, C3, C4, and C7.

\subsubsection{Task Success}

To address this question, we compare task success rates between control and treatment groups.

Experiment~1 used Fisher's exact test on session-level pass/fail (4/4 vs.\ 5/5 sessions above the 50\% threshold; see \S\ref{subsec:scoring-validation}); Mann-Whitney columns are shown for consistency. Experiment~2 produced a complete ceiling effect:
all ten runs scored 7/7, rendering the test uninformative ($U{=}12.5$, $p{=}1.000$,
$r{=}\text{n/a}$).\footnote{$\dagger$ Effect size undefined; all runs scored identically.}
Experiment~3 revealed rubric-runner co-design failure: criteria C1, C5, and C6 scored 0/1
across both groups because the corresponding investigations were absent from the runner prompt.
The restricted analysis retaining only criteria C2, C3, C4, and C7 yields identical rank
ordering and $p$-value to the full-rubric analysis (control mean 0.500, treatment mean 0.600,
on the 0--1 normalized scale). Across all seven criteria, the full-rubric means are control
0.286 and treatment 0.343; the gap between full-rubric and restricted means confirms a
rubric-runner co-design failure rather than a treatment or capability signal.

In Experiment~1, five of six criteria reached 100\% pass rate in both groups; only criterion C5
(must-not constraint captured) discriminated between groups: 50\% control, 80\% treatment (see \autoref{tab:results}).

\subsubsection{Experiment 4}
\label{subsec:exp4results}

Experiment~4 produced a second calibration failure of a different kind. Judge~1 (blind Claude Haiku~4.5) gave control a mean of 4.2/7 and treatment
4.0/7 (Mann-Whitney $U{=}15.0$, exact two-sided $p{=}1.0$, rank-biserial $r{=}-0.20$). Six of seven criteria sat at floor (C1, C2) or ceiling (C3, C5, C6, C7) in both
groups; the only criterion with variance, C4, differs by a single run. A second judge (Claude Sonnet~4.6, temperature 0.0), given the rubric and the session output only,
disagreed with Judge~1 on 10 of 70 criterion decisions, all of them fails of Judge~1 passes (C5 in 8 of 10 sessions); session-level agreement was 20\% ($\kappa{=}0.00$).
Exp4 therefore shows that revising the runner prompt did not by itself produce headroom, and that an unvalidated LLM judge can pass a criterion the second judge rejects
in most sessions. Neither result is evidence about prompt repetition in either direction. Together with Exp1--3, it set three requirements for Part~II: headroom measured
before the main run, executable scoring, and a pilot gate with a defined outcome for both floor and ceiling.

\subsubsection{Token and Message Efficiency}

As a secondary, exploratory outcome measure, we compare token and message counts between conditions.

In Experiment~1, treatment agents consumed 30.6\% fewer total tokens than control
(median 1{,}032{,}311 vs.\ 716{,}324); treatment sessions converged in fewer turns (see \autoref{tab:efficiency}).
In Experiment~2, treatment agents used 7.2\% more total tokens than control
(median 703{,}993 vs.\ 755{,}144); sessions were already saturated and no turns were eliminated,
so the doubled prompt added net input overhead. In Experiment~3, treatment agents
consumed 19.9\% more total tokens than control (median 41{,}880 vs.\ 50{,}220); sessions ran to a median
of 70 messages (control) and 82 messages (treatment) with no turns saved, so the doubled prompt imposed pure input overhead with no performance gain.

\subsection{Discussion of the Pilot Series}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsubsection{Mechanism Mismatch}

\textbf{Design choices relative to \citet{leviathan2025}.} Their intervention duplicates the entire user query in single-turn prompts across seven non-reasoning models, selecting benchmarks with substantial headroom below ceiling (e.g., 21\% baseline on NameIndex). We deliberately test a different regime: duplication targets the delegate system prompt, tasks are multi-step tool-loop sessions, a single model tier is used for cost control, and tasks were drawn from open, unimplemented issues without baseline-difficulty prescreening (Part~II adds that prescreening). Both calibration failure modes observed here (ceiling in Exp1--2, floor in Exp3) would have been excluded by headroom-first task selection.

Prompt repetition enables each token in \texttt{<QUERY>} to attend to every other token in a single causal pass, a mechanism effective only for single-turn inputs where the full context fits in one forward pass; in multi-step loops, the instruction's relative weight dissipates as context grows \citep{leviathan2025}.
By the time agents in Experiments~1 and~2 had exchanged 140--200 messages, the duplicated
instruction constituted a diminishing fraction of total context.

\subsubsection{Why Do the Results Diverge from Leviathan et al.?}

Three factors interact. First, mechanism: their gains arise at prefill time because causal attention leaves early query tokens blind to later ones \citep{leviathan2025}; duplicating a single-turn query grants every token a second pass over the full context. A system prompt, by contrast, remains fully attended throughout the session, and by messages 140--200 the duplicated block is a negligible fraction of accumulated context. Second, headroom: their gains concentrate where baseline accuracy is low; our baselines sat at ceiling (Exp1--2) or at a structural floor (Exp3). Third, agents already restate and re-read instructions via the tool-loop context, analogous to the reasoning-model case where repetition adds little.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/session-length-crossover.png}
\caption{\small \textbf{Prompt repetition token impact vs.\ session length.} Each point is one experiment; x-axis is mean control session length (messages), y-axis is token delta (\%, treatment vs.\ control), point size scales with mean control token volume. Turn delta is annotated below each point. Token savings require turn reduction; pure overhead accrues when turn count is unchanged. (Colors use the Okabe-Ito colorblind-safe palette.)}
\label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure}

The direction of the token delta is therefore a function of session length: in long-session
experiments, turn savings dominate; in short-session experiments, prompt overhead dominates. (\autoref{fig:crossover}).

Let $p$ be the prompt token count and $\bar{c}$ the mean tokens per message. A repeated
prompt adds $p$ tokens to every prefill. Each avoided turn eliminates $\bar{c}$ tokens of
accumulated context, where $\bar{c}$ grows with session length. The crossover point $L^*$
satisfies $p = \bar{c}(L^* - 1)$, giving $L^* \approx p/\bar{c} + 1$: sessions longer than
$L^*$ yield a net token saving; sessions shorter than $L^*$ incur overhead. For Experiment~3 ($p \approx 950$, $\bar{c} \approx
600$ tokens/message, from median totals of 41{,}880 and 50{,}220 tokens over 70 and 82 messages), $L^* \approx 950/600 + 1 \approx 2.6$ messages, well below the observed 70- and 82-message medians.
Yet treatment agents still consumed more tokens, because repeated instructions did not reduce
turn count: agents could not complete structurally unreachable criteria regardless of
instruction emphasis, so the turn-count assumption underlying $L^*$ does not hold under
rubric-runner misalignment.

\subsubsection{Rubric-Runner Co-design Failure}
\label{subsec:codesign}

A criterion is \emph{structurally unreachable} if no instruction in the runner prompt tasks the corresponding investigation (defined in \S\ref{sec:amendments}). The resulting floor effect is not an agent signal; it is an evaluation design artifact.
Experiment~3 exemplifies this failure mode. Criteria C1, C5, and C6 required agents to inspect
ABI compatibility constraints, node-kind taxonomy sources, and struct field layouts; none of
these investigations appeared in the runner prompt, and both groups scored 0/1 on all three
criteria.

We propose a rubric-runner alignment score $A = k_r / k$ where $k$ is the total number of
rubric criteria and $k_r$ is the number of criteria traceable to at least one explicit
instruction in the runner prompt. For Experiment~3, $A{=}4/7{=}0.57$; for Experiments~1
and~2, $A{=}1.0$. We recommend $A{=}1.0$ as a necessary (though not sufficient)
pre-registration gate: any criterion with $A{<}1.0$ should be revised or removed before
sealing the rubric. This eliminates a class of evaluation failures endemic to LLM-agent pipelines where rubric authors and prompt authors operate independently.

\citet{midolo2026guidelines} derive ten prompt improvement guidelines for code generation via iterative test-driven refinement, finding that explicitly specifying output post-conditions and I/O contracts produces the largest gains in test-pass rates, while structural repetition does not appear among the improvement patterns. This corroborates the rubric-runner alignment principle advanced here: traceability, not repetition count, is the operative lever, as diagnosed post hoc in Experiment~3.

\subsubsection{Infrastructure Confounds}
\label{subsec:infrastructure}

The Goose agent framework enforces a concurrency cap via
\texttt{GOOSE\_MAX\_BACKGROUND\_TASKS} (defaults to~5; configurable since Goose
1.29\footnote{\url{https://github.com/aaif-goose/goose/pull/7940}}).
In Experiment~2, this cap silently serialized ten delegates into two batches
approximately four minutes apart, introducing a temporal gap not anticipated in the
pre-registration. This serialization does not affect the statistical conclusion, but it illustrates a broader risk. Researchers should explicitly log concurrency caps and batch boundaries; undocumented serialization can bias latency measurements and, in non-ceiling experiments, interact with task difficulty.

\subsubsection{What the Pilots Require of a Confirmatory Test}

First, $n{=}5$ per group is
underpowered for medium effects; a powered replication with tasks whose
baseline score leaves headroom is needed to determine whether prompt repetition
produces any signal once ceiling and floor artifacts are removed. Second,
a single LLM judge cannot detect systematic scorer bias, and the Exp4 second-judge
check shows the bias can be large; executable scoring removes the judge from the
outcome. Third, all Haiku delegates were of one tier; other models may exhibit
different context-weighting dynamics under prompt repetition, so model should be a
factor rather than a constant. Part~II is designed on these three requirements.

\section{Part II: Pre-registered Confirmatory Experiment (Experiment 5)}

\subsection{Design}
\label{subsec:exp5design}

\subsubsection{Research Question and Hypothesis}

Does a verbatim second copy of the task instructions improve the executable task-success score of non-reasoning LLM agents on the PHP grammar scout task (\texttt{aptu-coder\#1864}),\footnote{\url{https://github.com/clouatre-labs/aptu-coder/issues/1864}} and does the answer depend on the model?
The pre-registered hypothesis is no effect of verbatim instruction repetition on the executable task-success score, for each model; the test has no assumed direction.
\textbf{Model as a factor.} Two delegate models, \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} and \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}, each crossed with the two arms; the per-model effect is the unit of analysis and the difference between the two per-model effects is reported as an exploratory interaction, not a confirmatory test. The non-reasoning claim rests on model choice: no reasoning or thinking effort is enabled through the framework, and thinking tokens are not measured (Section~\ref{subsec:exp5limits}).

\subsubsection{Task, Arms and Size}

The task is the Scout research task of Part~I, re-targeted to adding PHP language support through tree-sitter-php in a pinned clone of \texttt{aptu-coder} (commit \texttt{132ea687}), with the issue thread as the only permitted task-specific external input. The control arm carries one copy of the fixed prompt body in the user prompt; the treatment arm carries the same user prompt plus a byte-verbatim second copy of the body in the delegate instructions. The recipe text is generated by one script for both arms, so the arms differ only in that copy. Size: 2 models $\times$ 2 arms $\times$ $n{=}20$, 80 runs in total, executed in plan order, interleaved within model, with no early stopping, optional extension or replacement of failed runs.

\subsubsection{Pre-registration}

The protocol (research question, outcome, exclusions, statistics, seed, cost table and pooling estimator) was deposited as the pre-registration version of the dataset record, \href{\preregurl}{DOI:\preregdoi}, published on 2026-11-27 before the first main-run spawn; the label map was sealed after the deposit and before any run finished. One deviation from the protocol's timing is recorded in Section~\ref{subsec:exp5deviations}.

\subsubsection{Headroom Evidence}

Headroom was measured before the main run rather than assumed. Two earlier pilots were discarded as evidence: the first, without containment, reached the closed predecessor issue in two runs and scored at ceiling on an earlier 5-point scale; the second, with web search available, was contaminated when a search response returned that issue. The fenced re-pilot (four runs, the main-run instrument, the executable scorer) is the pre-registered evidence; its per-run scores are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:headroom}. On the primary scale, with the pre-registered band of acceptable control values, the \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} control fell below the band floor and the \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} control fell inside the band. The gate verdict is FLOOR, and the low \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} control is reported here as a model-level finding rather than treated as a stop condition: the task is kept, both models are run, and the per-model effect on \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} may be bounded by the scale rather than by repetition.

\subsubsection{Executable Scoring}

Part~II removes the LLM judge. Criteria C3 to C7 are scored as fractional values by a program from the delegate's output file: the element query draft and defuse query draft are compiled against the vendored tree-sitter-php grammar and matched on a fixture corpus (C3, C5), and the remaining criteria are counted from the literal artifacts of the output (C4, C6, C7). C1 and C2 are not executable offline and stay outside every score.
The primary outcome is \texttt{primary\_total = C3 + C4 + C5} on the 0 to 3 scale, taken as the scorer emits it and never recomputed. Because C5 was at the floor in every pilot run, \texttt{primary\_total} minus C5 (0 to 2) was declared in advance as a co-reported outcome and appears next to the primary outcome in every table. C6 and C7 are secondary outcomes: both were at ceiling in every run of the first pilot because the runner prompt enumerated the wiring facts literally, which removed them from the primary score and from the prompt.

\subsubsection{Execution Settings}

Goose \goosever{} for every run; provider Anthropic; temperature identical to Part~I; extensions \texttt{developer} and \texttt{context7} only; a turn cap of 60 for every run in both arms; concurrency 2. Containment: no web search extension, the clone's remote is removed after checkout so nothing can be fetched past the pin, and the delegate's \texttt{gh} is a shim that passes the task issue, the grammar repository's API and version or auth queries and blocks everything else, logging each blocked call. Prompt caching is on for both arms, so cost is reported cache-adjusted (Section~\ref{subsec:exp5cost}). Scorer and analysis code are sealed by tree hash in the label map at run time.

\subsubsection{Statistical Analysis}

Per model: Mann-Whitney $U$, two-tailed, at a significance level of 5\%, on \texttt{primary\_total}, with the same test on the co-reported outcome alongside. Effect: rank-biserial $r$ with Kerby's sign convention, treatment as $x$, so positive values favor treatment. Interval: seeded bootstrap, 1000 resamples per model, percentile method, seed 20261127, the same seed for both outcomes. Pooling: the unweighted mean of the two per-model $r$ values, never a pooling of raw scores across models. Interaction: the difference between the two per-model $r$ values, labelled exploratory.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Construct Validity}

Four aspects of the rubric design threaten construct validity.
First, Section~\ref{subsec:codesign} documents a rubric-runner co-design failure in Part~I: three of seven criteria in Experiment~3 scored 0/1 because the runner prompt lacked instructions for the required investigations. As a result, those criteria measure runner-prompt completeness rather than agent capability, limiting our ability to conclude that prompt repetition affects criterion-level pass rates on structurally unreachable items. Part~II removed the failure by measuring headroom before the main run and by scoring the artifacts the prompt literally requires.
Second, the binary pass/fail granularity of each criterion (chosen to enable blinded automated scoring across 30 sessions; polytomous scales would require judge calibration beyond the project's budget) compresses within-condition variance, reducing sensitivity to partial improvements that a polytomous or continuous scoring scheme might detect.
Third, scoring stability across alternative rubric phrasings was not tested in Part~I; a replication with criteria rewritten for full rubric-runner alignment ($A{=}1.0$, \S\ref{sec:discussion}) would test whether the observed effects hold under corrected instructions. Fourth, the primary scale of Part~II is bounded at 0 and 3, and its C5 component stayed near the floor, so effects that require headroom on C5 cannot be detected with this task.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

Several uncontrolled sources of variance limit internal validity.
First, Goose version 1.25.0 was used for Experiments~1 and~2 while version 1.31.1 was used for Experiment~3; version-specific behavioral changes in agent scheduling or tool-handling logic cannot be ruled out as confounds.
Second, three individual runs in Experiment~3 fell back to Bedrock due to stream-decode errors and Vertex AI rate limits before retrying successfully on Vertex; all token and score data derive from the successful Vertex runs, but the transient provider switch cannot be ruled out as a minor infrastructure variable.
Third, in Part~I a single LLM judge (Claude Haiku~4.5) performed all rubric scoring with no human calibration. The post hoc second-judge check (\S\ref{subsec:scoring-validation}) was a score-reconstruction exercise, not an independent re-scoring, so it does not establish that the scores are reproducible; whether they are reproducible across judges, LLM or human, is untested, and a human--model cross-validation remains future work. In Experiment~1 the scorer also knew each run's condition (\S\ref{sec:preregistration}), so condition-related scoring bias cannot be excluded for that experiment. This threat is confined to Part~I: Part~II is scored by an executable program with no judge in the loop.
Fourth, task ordering within each session was not randomized or counterbalanced. Order effects such as fatigue, learning, or context carry-over are therefore uncontrolled, limiting our ability to attribute score differences solely to the treatment.
Fifth, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:infrastructure}, the \texttt{GOOSE\_MAX\_BACKGROUND\_TASKS} concurrency cap serialized Experiment~2 delegates into two batches; while this did not affect the statistical conclusion in the present ceiling-affected data, it limits our ability to conclude that latency or delegation dynamics were uniform across the experiment.
Sixth, in Part~II the contamination rule is mechanical and over-inclusive: two excluded runs were flagged for a PHP snippet, not a network call, and one for reading the fence's own source. The exclusions were applied as pre-registered and the all-runs sensitivity table (Table~\ref{tab:exp5s1}) does not change the conclusion.

\subsection{External Validity}

The experimental design constrains external validity along four dimensions.
First, only four software-engineering tasks were sampled (session-ID refactoring, tree-sitter scanner implementation, Kotlin grammar synthesis, and PHP grammar synthesis for the Exp4 and Exp5 variants), which limits our ability to generalize to the broader distribution of SWE-bench-style tasks or to non-engineering domains such as data analysis or creative writing.
Second, Part~I used a single Haiku model at a single temperature ($0.5$; chosen as a compromise between eliminating within-condition variance at temperature 0 and inflating task-failure noise at $n{=}5$). Part~II adds a second model as a factor, but both are two generations of one small tier from one vendor, so results may not generalize to reasoning models, larger tiers, or other providers.
Third, a single agentic framework (Goose) was used; framework-specific features such as delegate spawning semantics or context-window boundaries may interact with prompt-repetition effects in ways that limit generalizability to other frameworks such as LangGraph or CrewAI.
Fourth, the lab setting used pre-defined tasks, isolated repositories, and scripted pipeline execution. This limits our ability to conclude that the null results hold in production pipelines, which face variable task difficulty, real-time API failures, and human-in-the-loop interruptions.

\subsection{Conclusion Validity}

Statistical power and design choices constrain conclusion validity.
The Part~I sample size of $n{=}5$ per condition was underpowered for medium effects (Cohen's $d \approx 0.5$); the pilots were powered for large effects only, which limits our ability to conclude that prompt repetition has no practically meaningful effect at smaller effect sizes. Part~II, with $n{=}20$ per arm and model, narrows the per-model intervals to about half a point of $r$ on either side of zero but still cannot exclude small effects.
No multiple-comparison correction was applied across the Part~I experiments, inflating the family-wise error rate; Part~II runs two pre-registered tests, one per model.
Ceiling effects in Experiments~1, 2 and~4 compress variance and further reduce statistical power, making true effects harder to detect even if they exist.
Finally, Experiment~1 was not fully pre-registered: the statistical test was not pre-specified. This partial pre-registration mitigates but does not eliminate the risk of HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known), and is one reason the whole pilot series is labelled exploratory; only Experiment~5 is confirmatory, and its one timing deviation is reported in Section~\ref{subsec:exp5deviations}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A pre-registered experiment with two non-reasoning models, $n{=}20$ per arm and model, and executable scoring finds no effect of prompt repetition on the task success of LLM agents executing a structured software-engineering task in multi-step pipelines: the per-model rank-biserial effects are $0.1667$ and $-0.0069$, both bootstrap intervals include zero, and the co-reported outcome and the all-runs sensitivity table agree. The exploratory pilots that motivated the design add two calibration failure modes (ceiling in Exp1, Exp2 and Exp4; rubric-runner co-design failure in Exp3), a judge whose passes a second judge rejected in most Exp4 sessions, and the containment without which the task's predecessor issue leaked into pilot runs. The evidence points to rubric-runner alignment, headroom measurement and executable scoring as the higher-leverage design investments over instruction emphasis. These findings do not extend to the positional-retrieval and multiple-choice settings where prompt repetition's gains are established, nor to reasoning models.
